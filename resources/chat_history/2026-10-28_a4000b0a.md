## A. Fix these first (an examiner will likely catch them)

**1. Your LCOE is understated by about 30%.** [simulator.py:254](src/simulator.py#L254) computes `NPC / (served × 15)`. That spreads the cost over 15 years without discounting the energy. HOMER, the tool the paper uses, computes `NPC × CRF / annual served`. I re-ran your optimum:
- As coded now: **₹9.63/kWh**
- With the capital recovery factor (CRF = 0.0945): **₹13.65/kWh**

The corrected figure falls inside the paper's off-grid range of ₹11.96–18.47. Slide 8 currently explains why yours comes out lower, so that argument needs rewriting.

**2. Salvage value is missing.** PV lasts 25 years but the project runs 15, so 10 years of PV life are left at the end. For your optimum that leftover value is about **₹35 M, or 7.6% of NPC**. Leaving it out makes PV look more expensive than it is, which tilts the PV-versus-battery trade-off. The year-8.4 battery replacement also has leftover life at year 15.

**3. The case for the supercapacitor is weak in the current design.** Your battery bank is 16.8 MWh. The 93 kW transient is **0.0055C** for that bank, and its discharge limit is about 9 MW. The batteries can absorb this step easily, so an examiner will ask why a supercapacitor is there at all. Two ways to make the case stronger:
- Argue that the supercapacitor takes the high-frequency current that would otherwise cause small, rapid battery cycles, so the battery lasts longer. Your planned one-minute model plus rainflow counting is exactly how you'd measure this.
- Compare against a lithium-ion battery, which is sized closer to its power rating. Its power-to-energy ratio makes the supercapacitor matter more.

**4. Motor starting current isn't in the transient.** [prepare_data.py](src/prepare_data.py) builds the step from rated kW. Induction motors started direct-on-line draw roughly 5–7× rated current. That applies to your compressors, pumps and M-G sets, and it's usually the real worst case.

**5. Smaller modelling gaps:**
- Converter efficiency (0.95) is defined in [costs.py](src/costs.py) but never used in the hourly dispatch.
- Battery charge and discharge current limits are defined but never enforced.
- The 10% load growth (`load_growth_per_year`) and the grid tariffs are defined but never used.
- `TRANSIENT_J = 465_000` is hard-coded in [simulator.py:44](src/simulator.py#L44) instead of read from `transient_spec.csv`.

## B. Additions that would strengthen the thesis (most valuable first)

1. **Grid-connected scenarios.** A fully off-grid system for two departments, with 3.5 MW of PV against a 644 kW peak, isn't very realistic. The paper covers both off-grid and grid-connected cases, and your tariffs are already in `ECON`. 2. **Rooftop and land area limit.** 3.5 MW of PV needs roughly 20,000–25,000 m². That is probably more than the Mechanical and EEE roofs can hold. Add an area limit, plus land cost, which the CERC capital cost doesn't include. The optimum may change.

3. **Shifting flexible loads into solar hours.** This is where your equipment-level load model gives you something unique. Pumping, laundry, compressors and the foundry could move into daylight hours. Measure how much battery that saves. Few campus microgrid papers can do this, because they don't have equipment-level data.

4. **Sensitivity and uncertainty analysis.** Your own slide 10 shows the cost basin is flat. Add:

5. **Lithium-ion (LFP) compared with lead-acid, at current prices.** Your costs are 2016-17 rupees, and lead-acid looks dated for a 2026 thesis. Running both 2016 and current prices is a result in itself.

6. **Multi-objective optimisation.** Use NSGA-II for NPC against LPSP, and add CO₂ as a third objective using the CEA grid emission factor. A Pareto front fits a flat basin better than a single optimum does.

9. **Validate against an independent tool.** Check the PV model against `pvlib`, and run one design in HOMER Pro (trial version) for comparison.

10. **Reliability beyond LPSP.** Report loss-of-load hours, and treat critical loads (NMR, ULT freezers, HPC) separately so they're served before anything else is.
